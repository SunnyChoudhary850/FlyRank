Content Refresh Opportunity Scoring

Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring — FlyRank ML Internship Capstone

Abstract

Content teams cannot manually review thousands of pages to identify which are declining and need attention. This paper asks whether a simple model, trained on visibility signals from a real content-performance dataset, can rank declining pages more reliably than a basic staleness rule. Using a grouped, client-holdout validation split, a Logistic Regression model reached 0.86 precision@50, compared to 0.56 for a staleness-only baseline. Average position and total impressions were the strongest predictors, far more than content age. The result supports a decision-support tool for prioritizing content review, not a fully automated action system.

Introduction

The decision this work supports: given limited review capacity, which content pages should a team look at first? Reviewing every page manually does not scale past a small portfolio, so a ranking tool that surfaces the most likely-declining pages first has direct, practical value if it is demonstrably better than guessing or a simple rule of thumb.

Data

Data comes from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (Hugging Face, gated access), specifically the dim_content and fact_content_daily_performance tables. The feature window used is a single mid-panel month (March 2026), with the label derived by comparing against the following month (April 2026). The sealed final month of the panel was deliberately excluded from all feature and label construction, per the dataset's own guidance, since it is reserved as an unseen test window.

No client names, domains, raw queries, or credentials appear anywhere in this paper or the underlying notebooks — all identifiers are pre-anonymized hashes provided by the dataset itself.

Methodology

Label definition: a page is labeled "declining" if its April impressions are lower than its March impressions — an observed, comparative signal, not a causal diagnosis of why a page declined.

Features: total impressions, average search position, days with recorded activity, total clicks, and total sessions (all from the March feature window), plus days since last content update.

Baseline: a simple rule ranking pages purely by staleness (days since last update).

Validation design: a grouped train/test split by client, not a random split — this tests whether the model generalizes to clients it has never seen, rather than partially memorizing familiar clients. A direct comparison confirmed this matters: a random split scored 0.80 precision@50, while the grouped split scored 0.86 — different numbers measuring meaningfully different things, not simply "better" or "worse" in one direction.

Leakage check: features were audited for correlation with the label-derivation column itself. One feature (total March impressions) correlated 0.87 with the following month's impressions — not strict leakage, since both are genuinely known at prediction time, but a structural relationship worth naming: some of the model's apparent skill may reflect a simple regression-to-the-mean effect rather than a richer decline pattern.

Results

ModelPrecision@50
Staleness-only baseline0.56
Random Forest0.74
Logistic Regression0.86

Logistic Regression outperformed Random Forest — a genuine, reported surprise, since the original plan expected Random Forest's added flexibility to help. With only six largely linear features, the simpler model appears sufficient, and the added complexity of Random Forest did not pay off here. This result is treated as evidence against reflexively choosing a more complex model.

Feature importance (from the Random Forest run) showed average position and total impressions dominating, together accounting for the majority of the model's decisions — staleness (days since update) ranked lowest. This means the model is primarily learning from visibility signals, not content age.

Limitations & Honest Framing

This is an observational comparison, not a causal claim — the model shows association between visibility signals and subsequent decline, not proof that changing one variable causes another. Results are directional and decision-support in nature: they indicate which pages are more likely worth a human reviewer's time, not a certified diagnosis of any single page. The feature window covers one month only, so seasonal effects are not captured. The label is a proxy (a fall in impressions), not a verified measurement of "why" a page declined. No claim is made about Google's ranking algorithm or about causal refresh impact.

Ranked Recommendations

The model output was turned into an action playbook with three tiers (REFRESH_PRIORITY, REVIEW_RECOMMENDED, MONITOR) and reason codes (WEAK_POSITION, LOW_VISIBILITY, STALE_CONTENT, GENERAL_RISK). A raw, purely score-ranked queue was found to concentrate almost entirely on a single client in initial testing — a real usability flaw that would have starved the rest of a real portfolio of review attention. The queue was corrected by capping candidates per client before re-ranking, producing a genuinely balanced recommendation set. The playbook explicitly states what must never be automated: no page should be auto-unpublished, auto-rewritten, or judged as low-quality based on this score alone — every flagged page requires human review before action.
